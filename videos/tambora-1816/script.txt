Imagine waking up in July, expecting summer… only to find snow falling from the sky. In 1816, this really happened in parts of North America and Europe.
But the cause was thousands of kilometres away. In April 1815, Mount Tambora, in present-day Indonesia, unleashed one of the most powerful volcanic eruptions ever recorded.
The eruption sent enormous amounts of sulfur dioxide high into the atmosphere. There, it transformed into tiny sulfate aerosols, which spread through the air and reflected some incoming sunlight back into space.
Less solar energy reached the surface. Temperatures fell, and weather patterns shifted. The effects were especially severe across parts of the Northern Hemisphere.
In 1816, parts of Europe and North America experienced unusual cold, persistent rain, and even summer snowfall. Crops struggled, harvests failed, and food became harder to find.
Communities faced hardship as food shortages and rising prices added to the crisis. A volcanic eruption thousands of kilometres away had consequences far beyond its birthplace.
The year became known as the Year Without a Summer. It was a powerful reminder that one eruption can influence the climate far beyond the volcano itself.
